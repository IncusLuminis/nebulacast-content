A wide, cinematic view of the Milky Way galaxy seen edge-on from a slight angle above the disk — same setting and palette family as the companion illustration, but this time built to read, at a glance, as "an enormous number of hidden objects" rather than a faint hint of a few. The galaxy's thin disk is rendered as a bright, warm band of dense starlight (soft golds and pale blues), sharply concentrated near the mid-plane. Surrounding and extending far above and below this luminous disk — reaching roughly 2 to 3 times the disk's own thickness — is a vast, densely populated halo volume filled with countless small, faint gravitational-lensing distortions: tiny points where background starlight is subtly bent, warped, or dimmed. These are not glowing objects and never have an accretion disk or halo of light around them individually — each is only a small, cool-toned ripple or micro-arc in the starfield, near-invisible on its own. The effect comes from sheer repetition and density: hundreds of these faint distortion-points scattered throughout the halo, noticeably denser near the disk plane and thinning out with height, so the eye reads "a teeming, unseen population" the way a dense star field reads "countless stars" — but rendered as absences and warps in the light rather than light sources themselves. No literal black circles, no silhouetted spheres, no accretion glow. Deep space background, sparse ordinary distant stars for scale contrast against the much denser field of dark distortion-points. Color palette: warm amber/white for the visible stellar disk, cool deep indigo and near-black for the halo volume, faint teal-white micro-lensing specks as the only signature of the hidden objects, slightly more numerous/saturated than the companion image to carry the "abundance" idea. Mood: vast, quiet, faintly overwhelming — the unmistakable sense that the darkness is far more crowded than the light. Style: realistic space art, painterly but scientifically grounded (ESA/NASA concept-illustration register), no text, no UI elements, no accretion-disk clichés, no literal black holes drawn as solid dark spheres.
